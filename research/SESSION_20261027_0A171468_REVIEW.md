## Why might H33 have done well, and can a new method beat it?

The archival forensic account reports a **37,654-dot** off-catalogue H33 descendant of a denser parent, rather than a wholly new independent support; its spatial support overlaps the other Focus-9 examples heavily. With the shared distance-weighted Tversky metric, `DTI = TPw / (TPw + 0.2 FPw + 0.8 FNw)`, and a 300 m triangular kernel, thinning redundant mass and choosing locations that cover previously missed faults could improve the precision–recall tradeoff. Conversely, removing a near-catalogue dot can lose real hidden-fault credit: visible faults are masked pixel-exactly, not as a whole 300 m collar, and kernels centered three pixels apart can overlap. Thus dot count, collar width, far-field share, and family correlation are **hypotheses**, not proven causes of the owner-reported 0.2778. The hidden organizer truth, a portal receipt, and controlled same-support ablations are unavailable. See the [interpretation review](H33_INTERPRETATION_REVIEW.md) and [shared metric](../vendor/template66/gems57/metric.py).

A genuinely different physics-informed method **could** in principle beat H33; this work has not shown that it does. A scientifically credible attempt would preregister a single lane, use the same segment-buffered split and paired confidence intervals against H33 and same-split best, perform per-feature leakage canaries (AUC above 0.90 is a stop), and enforce both preplacement surface and final-dot registry comparisons. No projection, past proxy performance or byte-distinct file establishes such an improvement. Under today's literal witness rule, even a better model cannot pass uniqueness.
